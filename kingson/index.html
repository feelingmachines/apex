Research

Feeling, vulnerability, and artificial agents

Living organisms must keep themselves alive. With Antonio Damasio, I proposed that homeostasis, the regulation of internal conditions, could provide a basis for artificial motivation and feeling-like behavior. My current work tests these ideas in simulation: agents’ energy and water levels affect their ability to act, and they anticipate how their choices will affect their bodies.

I ask how this embodied vulnerability changes behavior under threat, and how an agent’s beliefs about its own body influence the risks it takes. Learning useful models of those consequences remains an open research problem.

Homeostasis, learning, and cooperation

With Naoto Yoshida, I study how sharing internal states can support helping behavior in simulated agents. With Damasio and Hartmut Neven, I developed neural networks that regulate their own learning rates to adapt when the relationship between inputs and labels changes.

How the senses become a world

My doctoral and postdoctoral research used brain imaging and machine learning to study how the brain represents objects across sight, sound, and touch. I also studied how seeing an object changes our ability to hear it, and how the brain represents the meaning of stories across languages.

A little background

I first encountered Antonio Damasio’s The Feeling of What Happens in high school. That book helped set the direction of my scientific life. I later earned my PhD with him at USC, studying how the brain brings different senses together into a coherent world.

Since then, my work has taken me from neuroimaging to soft robotics and a research internship at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory. At Feeling Machines, I bring these threads together to study how bodily needs shape intelligence.

I remain an affiliate of USC’s Brain and Creativity Institute.

Short bio for organizers

Kingson Man is a neuroscientist and founder of Feeling Machines, where he studies homeostasis, vulnerability, and adaptive behavior in artificial agents. He earned his PhD at USC with Antonio Damasio. His research has appeared in Nature Machine Intelligence and Science Robotics, and is featured in Michael Pollan’s A World Appears and Valerio Jalongo’s documentary Wider than the Sky.

Clinical AI at City of Hope

Alongside Feeling Machines, I lead applied AI projects in oncology at City of Hope. I’m a Data Science Manager, with a courtesy appointment as Associate Professor at the Beckman Research Institute.

Research

At City of Hope, I lead work on HopeLLM, a clinical AI platform for extracting and retrieving information from oncology records. We evaluate model outputs with clinicians on real cases. Projects include clinical trial matching and RESiN, a method designed to examine every note before answering a clinical question.
